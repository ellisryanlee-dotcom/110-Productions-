# Diagnose, solve, value, price: selling outcomes instead of tools

**What:** A positioning and pricing framework for selling AI-built automations as business solutions rather than as technical deliverables. The core sequence is diagnose (find where a business is losing time, money, or focus), solve (build a system addressing that specific pain, not a generic showcase of technical capability), value (translate the fix into concrete numbers — hours saved, cost avoided, revenue enabled), and price (anchor the fee to a fraction of that quantified value rather than to hours worked or system complexity). The underlying math: estimate the process's current time cost (hours × hourly value of the person doing it × frequency), annualize it, then price the project as roughly 10–20% of that annual figure so the client sees a clear multiple return within the first year — with the target client experience being that the investment pays for itself within weeks and everything after is pure gain.

**Why it matters:** Framing an offer around the technology itself puts it in direct, commoditized price competition with anyone else who can build something similar; framing it around a specific, quantified business outcome makes the fee comparison irrelevant, because the client is evaluating it against the cost of the problem, not against another vendor's automation.

**The moves:**
1. In discovery, map the current manual process end to end: what triggers it, who does it, how long it takes, what it costs per week/month, and where the errors or bottlenecks are.
2. Convert that mapped process into a dollar figure: hours involved × the hourly value of the people doing it × how often it recurs, then annualize it.
3. Design the solution around directly addressing the most costly/frequent part of that mapped process, not around demonstrating impressive technical architecture.
4. Set the price as a fraction (roughly 10–20%) of the annualized savings figure, so the return is obviously multiples of the fee within the first year — and be prepared to walk a client through exactly how that number was derived if asked.
5. Present the transformation and what's included before ever stating the price, so the client is bought into the outcome first and the number lands as an investment relative to that outcome, not as a cost in isolation.
6. After delivery, keep tracking and periodically reporting the real measured impact (not just the projected one) — this is what turns a one-off project into a case study and opens the door to expansion work or a retainer.

**Watch out for:** Pricing purely by hours worked caps income regardless of value delivered and can make a genuinely valuable fast build look artificially expensive per hour — resist reverting to hourly framing once value-based numbers are available. Overpromising a specific ROI figure without having actually done the discovery math first undermines credibility if a client asks how the number was reached.

**Original example to invent:** The source's worked example was a sales-lead-qualification process costing roughly $38K a year, priced at $5,500. Writers should invent a different process and different numbers (e.g., a document-review workflow) and run the same diagnose→solve→value→price math with those invented figures.
